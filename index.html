    How to read this
    • Per decade to total: multiply by the number of decades. A trend of 0.5 °C per decade means about 1.5 °C over 30 years.
    • Trend: slope of a straight line through the yearly averages (Theil–Sen), per decade, after removing the seasonal cycle.
    • Significant: the change is unlikely to be chance (Mann–Kendall test, p < 0.05). Black dots on the map mark significant cells.
    • FDR correction: with many cells tested at once, some look significant by luck. The Benjamini–Hochberg correction limits this.
    • Years needed: the shortest time window ending in 2024 from which the trend is significant and stays significant for all longer windows. Grey means: not significant even with the full 44 years.
    • Year the trend became detectable: the first end year from which the trend since 1981 is significant for every later end year. It looks back; it is not a prediction.
    • Unusual months: months per year that are warmer/colder (or wetter/drier) than 90 % of the same calendar month in 1981–2010. By definition about 1.2 per year in that baseline; changes after it show how the extremes shifted. Whole year only.
    • Windows tested: all windows ending in 2024, from 44 years (1981–2024) down to a minimum of 10 years. Shorter windows are not tested because trends over fewer years are dominated by year-to-year noise. “≤ 10 years” on the map means: already significant in the shortest window tested.
    Limits of this tool
    • Values are reanalysis/model data from NASA POWER, not direct measurements. We compared them with measured country series: for temperature the yearly values match well in Germany, Sweden, Finland, Norway, Latvia and Lithuania, the UK, Spain, Italy and Greece (r = 0.93–0.99) and the trends agree within about a third (POWER somewhat lower in Spain, Italy and Norway). For precipitation the yearly values match in Germany, the UK and Ireland (r = 0.85, 0.90 and 0.89), but the trend direction agrees only in the UK and Ireland, not in Germany, so precipitation trends are read with care. Sunshine was checked in Germany and the UK (r = 0.91 and 0.75).
    • Each cell is a single grid point, not an area average. Neighbouring cells can differ because of local variability.
    • This tool describes past trends. It does not forecast: projections need climate models (e.g. CMIP6) and emission scenarios, and extending a straight line is not a forecast.
    • Only linear trends over 1981–2024 are tested. A significant trend does not tell us the cause.
    • Precipitation enters as monthly means. Single heavy-rain events cannot be seen in the monthly variables (the daily extremes group adds heavy-rain days and the longest dry spell), so “no significant trend” does not mean that nothing is changing. The “unusual months” variables look at the extremes of the monthly values (warmest/coldest, wettest/driest 10 % of a calendar month in 1981–2010), not at single days.
    • All time windows end in 2024; only the start year changes. The exception is the view “Year the trend became detectable”: there the start stays at 1981 and the end year moves, to show when a trend emerged. Other windows that end earlier (for example to look at a past pause) are not tested.
    • Soil moisture is not defined over the sea, so those cells stay empty.
    • Sunshine at the ground comes from two satellite products (SRB until 2000, CERES from 2001). Over Germany, POWER sits about 0.2 MJ/m²/day lower before 2001 than a fit to the DWD sunshine hours predicts, so part of its upward trend may come from the switch. Treat its trend as less certain than temperature.
    • Neighbouring cells see the same weather and are not independent. “315 of 315 cells” is therefore not 315 independent confirmations. The FDR correction handles many tests, not this dependence.
    • Cells are point samples every 2.5° (roughly 100–280 km). Mountains and coastlines are smoothed, so local values can be milder or stronger than at a weather station.
    • Land and sea are separated in the regional averages, but a 2.5° cell counts as land if its grid point lies on land, so coastal cells are approximate.
    • The 95 % range shown for a slope ignores autocorrelation. A separate check that allows for year-to-year autocorrelation (script autocorr_check.py in the project repository) shows which results survive it: soil moisture and relative humidity mostly do not.

    Putting it in context: the pre-industrial baseline

    Our data start in 1981, so this page cannot measure warming since the pre-industrial period (1850–1900). Roughly the first 130 years of warming lie before our record begins. The figures below are not computed here; they come from the Copernicus/WMO report European State of the Climate 2025 (ERA5 reanalysis) and are given for orientation only.

    • Europe is now about 2.5 °C warmer than in 1850–1900.
    • The world as a whole is about 1.4 °C warmer (the Paris Agreement limit is 1.5 °C).
    • Since 1980, Europe has warmed about twice as fast as the global average.

    What this means for the maps: the strong, significant warming trends on this page (since 1981) are the recent, accelerating part of a longer change. Different datasets, regions and reference periods give slightly different numbers, so do not add them to the trends shown on the maps. Sources: C3S/WMO, European State of the Climate 2025; report data notes (1850–1900 reference).

    Temperature matches well: the trends agree within 0.1 °C per decade in eight of nine countries or regions, and the largest difference is 0.16 °C per decade (Norway, +0.48 measured vs. +0.32 POWER). Precipitation matches in its yearly swings (r = 0.85 in Germany, 0.90 in the UK, 0.89 in Ireland), but the direction of the trend agrees in the UK and Ireland and not in Germany, so with three countries checked its colours should still be read with care. These are country means or averages of the grid points inside a country, not single stations. A single station is usually noisier than a smoothed grid cell, so a local user may need more years than the map shows; we did not test this.

    Glossary: variables, units and terms
    • Anomaly: a value minus the long-term average of the same calendar month, so the seasonal cycle does not hide the trend.
    • Theil–Sen slope: the median of all slopes between pairs of years. Robust against single extreme years.
    • p-value: probability of seeing a trend at least this strong if there were no real trend (Mann–Kendall test).
    • FDR (false discovery rate): correction for running one test per grid cell (Benjamini–Hochberg).
    • Per decade: change over 10 years in the unit of the variable.
    • Season: winter = December of the previous year plus January and February, so the first complete winter is 1982 (43 years). Summer = June to August.
    • Trend-to-noise ratio: the trend per decade divided by the year-to-year scatter around the fitted line (standard deviation of the residuals, whole period). Unit-free, so variables can be compared.

    Data and limitations

    • Modelled data, not stations. NASA POWER values come from the MERRA-2 reanalysis (and, for sunshine, from satellite-based SRB and CERES products). One value stands for a grid cell of 2.5° (roughly 100–280 km), so local weather, cities and mountains are not resolved.
    • Sunshine has a source break. The record joins SRB (until 2000) and CERES (from 2001). Trends in sunshine are therefore less certain than those in temperature, and the series starts in 1984.
    • Rain trends need care. A check against German station data matched year-to-year variation (r = 0.85) but not the sign of the trend, so precipitation trends should be read with care. The combined warming-and-precipitation view therefore does not show their direction.
    • “Years needed” is a look back, not a forecast. It uses the fixed record 1981–2024. It tells how long a trend had to run before it could be distinguished from year-to-year noise, not when a future trend will appear.
    • Statistics. Theil–Sen slopes, Mann–Kendall tests and a false-discovery-rate correction across cells. Year-to-year dependence was checked separately and reduces the number of detectable cells (for temperature 305 of 315 remain). The planner assumes independent years.
    • Daily extremes are experimental. Hot days, heavy-rain days and the longest dry spell come from modelled daily values of the same grid cells. A grid-cell maximum is usually lower than what a station records, and we did not validate daily extremes against stations. Read the change over time, not the absolute counts.
    • No causes, no projections. The page shows what the data reveal. It does not attribute trends to causes and does not predict the future.
